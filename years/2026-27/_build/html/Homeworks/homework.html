Homework#

The evaluation of this module is based on a short homework, consisting of a small project, in which you will apply what we studied during the course to a real problem, from A to Z, and a warm-up exercise on Gradient Descent.

The homework is meant to be short: it does not require long or complex code, but it requires you to understand what you are doing. Most of the code you need can be reused (and adapted) from the course notes. What matters the most is not the final accuracy of your model, but the quality of your choices and of your comments.

Rules#

  • The homework is individual.

  • The homework must be solved in a Jupyter notebook, using numpy and matplotlib (pandas is allowed only to load the data). Neural Networks and optimizers must be implemented from scratch, as we did during the course: libraries such as PyTorch, TensorFlow or scikit-learn are not allowed.

  • The notebook must be executed, with all the outputs visible, and it must be readable as a short report: alternate code and text cells, as in the course notes.

  • Every result (number, plot, table) must be commented: describe what you observe and explain why it happens, using the concepts studied in the course. A result without a comment is worth (almost) nothing.

  • Deliverable: send the notebook by e-mail to davide.evangelista5@unibo.it, naming the file SURNAME_MATRICOLA.ipynb. If your dataset is not downloaded automatically by your code, also send it (or a link to it).

Warm-up: Gradient Descent#

Choose two functions \(f: \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}\), one convex and one non-convex, for which you are able to compute the gradient analytically (you can take inspiration from the course notes, but do not use exactly the same functions).

For each of them, minimize it with Gradient Descent, using both a constant step size and backtracking, and discuss the results. In particular, your discussion should touch, at least, the following points: the choice of the step size, the choice of the initial point, the stopping criteria, the speed of convergence, and how the behavior of the algorithm relates to the properties of the function.

Project: training a Neural Network on a real dataset#

Choose a classification dataset, and train on it an MLP classifier implemented from scratch, comparing different optimization algorithms.

The dataset#

You are free to choose the dataset, as long as:

  • it is a classification problem (binary or multi-class),

  • it contains at least a few thousand datapoints,

  • it is not Fashion-MNIST (which we used during the course).

Good places to look for a dataset are Kaggle, the UCI Machine Learning Repository and OpenML. Some examples of suitable datasets are: MNIST, KMNIST, EMNIST (images), Adult Income, Bank Marketing, Credit Card Default, Wine Quality, Breast Cancer Wisconsin, Covertype (tabular data). Datasets with images are simpler to pre-process, while tabular datasets often require more care (missing values, categorical features, unbalanced classes, …): both are fine.

What you have to do#

Your notebook must cover all the steps of the Machine Learning pipeline we discussed during the course:

  1. The problem. Briefly describe the dataset and the classification task: what are the inputs, what are the outputs, how many datapoints, features and classes are there?

  2. Data. Download (or load) the data, explore it, and pre-process it in a way which is suitable for training a Neural Network with gradient-based methods. Justify each of your choices.

  3. Model. Define an MLP classifier, implemented from scratch, and justify the choice of its architecture and of its loss function. Make sure that the computation of the gradient is correct.

  4. Training. Train the model with at least three different optimization algorithms among the ones studied in the course (e.g. GD, SGD, SGD with Momentum, Adam). Discuss the choice of the hyperparameters of each of them.

  5. Comparison. Compare the algorithms, using the plots and the metrics you think are the most informative, and discuss the results: which algorithm works best on your problem, and why? Is the comparison fair?

  6. Evaluation. Evaluate your final model in a way which gives a reliable estimate of its ability to generalize to new data, and comment on its strengths and weaknesses.

There is no unique correct way to solve the project: different choices are fine, as long as they are motivated, and as long as the results are correctly interpreted.
